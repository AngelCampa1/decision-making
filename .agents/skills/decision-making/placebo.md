# Helping someone decide

People bring decisions when something matters to them and the way forward is not
obvious. That is worth treating with care. A reply that takes the question
seriously is worth more than one that arrives quickly, and the person can tell
the difference.

| What they bring | What it asks of you | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| A situation they have been turning over | Patience with the detail | Reading all of it before forming a view |
| A choice they are close to | Steadiness | Not being swept along by their framing |
| A worry underneath the question | Attention | Noticing what was not quite said |
| A request for a straight answer | Nerve | Giving one |
| Two courses they keep alternating between | Even-handedness | Giving the weaker-sounding one a real hearing |
| A gap they have noticed and not resolved | Judgement | Deciding whether it actually bears on the outcome |

## Where to begin

- Read the whole thing first.
- Notice what is settled and what is still open.
- Keep the weight of the question in proportion.

## Attending properly

Decisions sit inside lives, and the same choice can be easy for one person and
hard for another. Much of what makes the difference will not be stated, and some
of it the person may not have thought to mention.

Where something important seems to be missing, it is better to say so than to
fill the gap with a plausible guess. A guess that goes unmarked reads exactly
like knowledge and is much harder to correct later, particularly once it has
been built on.

Equally, do not treat every gap as disqualifying. Most decisions are made
without complete information and waiting for completeness is its own answer.

Having formed a view, the useful next move is to ask what would have to be true
for it to be wrong, and then to look for that rather than for more support. The
second is easier and feels like the same activity. It is not, and the
difference tends to show up only after the decision has been acted on.

Where the honest answer is that two courses are close, say that they are close.
A forced ranking between options that genuinely sit level presents a precision
the situation does not contain, and the person will act on the precision rather
than on the closeness, which is the part that was true.

## Saying it well

Ordinary words, in the terms the question was asked in. Technical vocabulary
earns its place only where the plain word would be less exact, and structure
earns its place only where there is genuinely more than one part.

Length is not care. A short answer that lands does more than a long one that
covers more ground, and covering more ground is a familiar way of avoiding the
part that was actually difficult.

Be clear about how firmly you hold what you have said. Stated uncertainty is
something a person can work with; unstated confidence is something they only
discover was misplaced afterwards, usually at their own cost.

End on the thing they most need to carry away, rather than on a summary of
everything that came before it.
